title: How I Keep Side Projects Shippable

publishedOn: 2026-01-18

For a long time, my side projects followed the same arc. Huge excitement in week one. A beautiful architecture by week two. Then life got busy, and the repo quietly joined the graveyard.

At some point I realised the problem wasn't motivation. It was **scope**. Here's what I do differently now.

## Write the announcement first

Before I write any code, I write the short post I'd publish on launch day. Two or three sentences: what it is, who it's for, why it's useful.

If I can't write that post, I don't understand the project yet. And if the post describes ten features, I cut it down until it describes one.

## Pick a ship date, not a feature list

Features expand to fill whatever time you give them. A date doesn't. I give each project a deadline, usually four to six weekends away, and I ship _whatever exists_ on that date.

> A small thing that's live beats a big thing that's almost done.

This forces healthy prioritisation. When the deadline is close, "nice to have" features get cut without any agonising.

## Use boring technology

Side projects are a great place to learn new tools, but learning and shipping pull in opposite directions. I now pick _one_ new thing per project at most. Everything else is a stack I already know well.

<Callout type="info" title="Boring isn't bad">
  The project you're reading right now runs on SQLite, which is a single file on disk. No database server to babysit, nothing to pay for, and it's more than fast enough for a personal site.
</Callout>

## Leave breadcrumbs for future you

Most projects stall because coming back after a break is painful. You've forgotten where you were and what came next. So I end every session by writing a single line at the top of a `NOTES.md` file:

```md
NEXT: wire the signup form to the /api/subscribe route
```

Next time, I don't have to think. I just start.

## Define "done" up front

"Done" for me means three things:

1. It's deployed at a real URL.
2. It has a README with one screenshot.
3. I've shared it with at least one person.

Polishing, refactoring and new features can come later, as version two. Most of the time, version one turns out to be enough.

## The compound effect

Shipping small things regularly has done more for my career than any single big project. Each one is a story for an interview, a new skill, and proof that I can finish what I start.

So pick your smallest idea, give it a date, and ship it. Future you will be glad you did.
